For a few days after the first march, a lot of shops in the centre of Milan did not open. The ones that did had wood panels over their windows. In the same streets, groups of people were chanting that they were antifascist.

This is my city, and it was sad to see it boarded up. The people on both sides of those panels pay the same bills: more for gas, more for electricity, more for rent, with wages that have barely moved in years. The bills are real. The diagnosis on the placards is wrong, and so is the way some people chose to make the point.

This autumn's bill comes from a war in the Gulf that closed the Strait of Hormuz, on top of the Russian gas Europe has mostly stopped buying since 2022. Behind that sit thirty years of flat wages, and a city where housing has outrun pay. None of that is fascism, and none of it gets better when a window gets smashed.

Here is what happened, where I stand on the violence, and what the numbers say about why life costs more.

## What happened

Students marched in several cities on Friday 16 October.[^students] The big march came the next day, Saturday 17 October. It was called from an Instagram account opened two weeks earlier by a new network born on social media, and its posters described it as a peaceful gathering of citizens. The demands were about everyday life: higher wages, safe work, lower prices, better healthcare and schools.[^ilpost] Many marchers were also against war and against military spending.[^aj]

In Milan it started peacefully. Near the Duomo and the Galleria, masked groups broke away, threw stones at the police, smashed shop windows, looted stores and set barricades on fire. Police answered with tear gas and water cannon. About fifty people were detained in Milan that day, half of them minors.[^day1] Many of the people breaking windows were teenagers who had organised on TikTok and in school and neighbourhood chats, and who used the bigger march as cover.[^ilpost]

The next day several hundred people in hoods were back around the Duomo, breaking windows and burning bins.[^day2] In Milan, police said 271 people were detained that Sunday, 23 of them minors, and 32 were arrested. Luxury shops in the centre stayed shut for fear of more.[^day3]

The network that called the first march condemned the riots and the looting. It blamed "isolated groups foreign to the mobilisation".[^ilpost]

A new group formed after that weekend and went the other way. Its announcement called for a "permanent mobilisation" every Saturday until the government falls. It told critics that "the violent ones are you, who denied us a future". Its private chat passed around a guide on avoiding identification and on what to do when police charge, and its page shared a video of goods it presented as loot from Milan's shops being handed to a homeless man, captioned "proletarian expropriation".[^open] Its manifesto says it has no leaders, so that there is no one for "repression and fascists" to target. It does not distance itself from the looting.[^avvenire]

The following Saturday, 24 October, Milan marched again, and this time largely in peace. Bologna, Turin, Florence and Rome did not. Bologna alone counted seventeen injured.[^oct24]

France had its own version on 17 October. The Interior Ministry counted more than 550,000 marchers, and most of them marched peacefully. Then came the looting and the fires. The town hall in Limoges was largely destroyed, and the French prime minister said 1,200 people had been arrested and 150 police officers hurt.[^france]

## Where I stand on the violence

The cause is legitimate. The violence is wrong. And whoever calls a march answers for what happens on it.

The bills really went up, and the rest of this post is about why.

The looting and the smashing are wrong, whoever did them. They are not understandable, not the product of exasperation, and not a footnote to a good cause. A smashed window does not lower anyone's gas bill.

The third point is the one I care about most. A movement that wants to speak for people is responsible for its own street. "Isolated groups foreign to the mobilisation" is not an answer. It describes a problem every organiser in Italy knows is coming, because masked groups have turned up at big marches for decades. Italian unions have run their own stewards, the servizio d'ordine, for exactly this kind of day. If you call a march, you plan who keeps it safe and where it ends. You say before it starts, and loudly afterwards, that whoever smashes a window is not with you. If you can bring a city into the street from an Instagram account opened two weeks earlier, you can use the same account to say that the balaclavas stay at home.

<Aside label="servizio d'ordine">
  The stewards that Italian unions and parties have long put along their own marches, to keep the route, the crowd and the end of the day under control.
</Aside>

This is not collective guilt. The student who marched peacefully is not responsible for the person in the balaclava next to them. But the people who call a march are responsible for how they deal with that person, and the answer can't be silence. It certainly can't be a meeting point that nobody knows until half an hour before, which no steward can plan around.

<PullQuote>A good cause does not stop being good because some of the people around it are violent. But the violence stays wrong, and the movement has to reject it out loud.</PullQuote>

The cost of getting this wrong is not abstract. A luxury brand can replace its glass by Monday. A bar or a phone shop on the same street is paying this autumn's electricity prices on thinner margins, and every evening the shutters stay down is lost takings and lost shifts for the people who work there.

The politics move too. Within two days the government had announced soldiers for Milan, and within three it had passed an emergency decree with a new crime of violent participation in a demonstration.[^decree] That is what the violence bought the movement: a conversation about public order instead of a conversation about bills.

## What war do they think they're fighting?

Then there was the chant.

Fascism has a meaning. The regime that ruled Italy from the 1920s abolished free elections and opposition parties, used organised violence to take power and keep it, and put every institution under one party. You can test a government against that.

Italy in 2026 is nowhere near it. Opposition parties campaign and win. Courts can and do overrule ministers. In March, voters rejected the government's own reform of the judiciary in a constitutional referendum, roughly 54 to 46, and the government said it accepted the result.[^referendum] Freedom House, which grades every country every year, rates Italy Free, at 87 out of 100.[^fh]

The longest record is V-Dem's electoral democracy index, which scores how free and fair a country's elections are, and the freedoms around them, from 0 to 1. Under the regime, Italy scored about 0.06. Today it scores 0.75, a little below Germany and France, and far above Hungary at 0.42.[^vdem]

The word "antifascist" also means two different things. Italy's constitution is antifascist by design. It was written after the war by the parties of the Resistance, and it bans the re-founding of the Fascist party.[^constitution] In that sense the word belongs to the whole Republic, including the people the chant was aimed at. The other meaning is a street identity that treats the police, the right, or capitalism itself as fascism, and treats confrontation as justified. The chant borrows the authority of the first meaning for the tactics of the second.

So what war? Not one against a regime. The fight that matters this autumn is against a gas price and a rent, and neither of those has a side you can smash.

## It's the gas

Start with the numbers. In September, Italian consumer prices were 4.2% higher than a year earlier, up from 3.3% in August. Leave out energy and fresh food and the rate was 1.6%. Energy on regulated tariffs was up 25.8% in a year, and energy on the free market 22.5%.[^istat] From 1 October the energy regulator raised electricity bills by 37.3% for the roughly three million households still on its regulated tariff, and blamed wholesale power prices pushed up by gas.[^bills]

Put Italy next to the rest of the euro area and one line stands out. Fuel for cars went up sharply everywhere, because oil is priced on a world market. Electricity and gas did not. In Italy they cost 19.1% more than a year earlier. In the euro area as a whole the figure was 6.9%, and in France 7.3%.[^hicp]

That shape matters. When core prices are rising at about 2%, the economy is not overheating and wages are not chasing prices. Something from outside is pushing on one part of the basket. The Bank of Italy reached the same verdict about the 2021–23 wave: energy and food drove it, shortages of parts played a smaller role, and there was no wage-price spiral.[^bdi] Daniela Fantozzi and Davide Zurlo split Italian core inflation into demand and supply shocks and reach a similar answer: demand mattered after the reopening, but the rate stayed high in 2022 mainly because of supply.[^fantozzi]

  Core inflation leaves out the prices that jump around the most, energy and food. It is the quickest read of whether price rises are spreading through the whole economy.
</Aside>

It also lands unevenly. Households that spend less faced 4.4% inflation in the third quarter, against 2.9% for households that spend more, because energy and food are a bigger share of a small budget.[^istat]

Gas matters even for people who never cook or heat with it, because of how electricity is priced. In the wholesale market, each hour's price is set by the most expensive plant needed to meet demand in that hour. Solar and wind offer power at close to nothing, since their fuel is free. Gas plants offer it at a high price and are switched on last. So the gas plant usually sets the price for everyone, including the solar farm.

A 2023 study measured how often each kind of plant set the price across Europe. In 2021, fossil plants set it 58% of the time while producing only 34% of the electricity. In Italy, fossil plants, mostly gas, set the price in 82% of hours, up from 59% in 2015. Over the same years the average Italian wholesale price went from €51 to €125 a megawatt-hour.[^zakeri] France, where nuclear plants set the price most of the time, sits at the other end. A 2026 study of Italian monthly data from 2021 to 2026 found the same link: gas prices drive the wholesale power price, and the power price then feeds into inflation and industrial output.[^mele]

You can see it in the prices themselves. From 2015 to 2020, European gas averaged about €16 a megawatt-hour, and Italian wholesale power about €50. When gas spiked in 2022, power went with it, to €544 in August. In September this year power was back above €200, and gas at €75.[^energy]

That's why a war on the other side of the world shows up on a Milan electricity bill within weeks.

## Who sells us cheap gas?

Here's the part a lot of people don't want to hear.

Until 2022, Russia was Italy's biggest supplier of gas, through a pipe. In 2021 Italy bought 29 billion cubic metres from Russia, 40% of all the gas it imported.[^russia] The fall since then was not a clean break. Gazprom itself cut deliveries during 2022 and stopped them in October that year.[^gazprom] Russian gas still made up 10% of imports in 2024. It has been close to nothing since the transit route through Ukraine closed on 1 January 2025: under one billion cubic metres last year, about 1% of demand.[^tarvisio] The gap was filled with more gas from Algeria and Azerbaijan by pipe, and with liquefied gas by ship from the United States and Qatar.[^eurostatgas]

Whoever made the call, losing Russian gas had a price.

Gas by ship is bought on a world market, where Asian buyers bid for the same cargoes. Before the war in the Gulf, European gas was already about twice its level of the late 2010s: €34 a megawatt-hour in January, against an average of €16 from 2015 to 2020.[^energy] And a shipping route can be closed. In late February, strikes between the United States, Israel and Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz, the route for about a fifth of the world's liquefied gas.[^ecb] Qatar, one of Italy's main suppliers, declared force majeure. Edison, which has a long-term contract to bring Qatari gas to Italy, about a tenth of the country's gas use, has seen 35 cargoes fail to arrive and expects none before December.[^edison] Qatar's share of Italy's imports fell from 11% last year to 3% in the first nine months of this one, and American ships went from 16% to almost a quarter.[^eurostatgas] Iran says the strait stays closed until its conditions are met.[^hormuz]

No Italian government decides whether the Strait of Hormuz is open.

Now put three wishes from the placards side by side: cheap energy, support for Ukraine, and no money for defence. They don't fit together. Russian gas by pipe was the cheap option, and without it the gap is filled by ship at world prices. So cheap energy and standing with Ukraine pull in opposite directions. Standing with Ukraine without spending on defence means asking someone else to pay for it. The only pair that holds together is cheap energy and no defence spending, and its price is Ukraine.

<PullQuote>Cheap energy, support for Ukraine, no money for defence. You can't have all three, and today you can't even have the first two together.</PullQuote>

Either we accept the cost, or we accept what the alternative means for Ukraine.

The way out is to need less gas from anyone. That means renewables, storage and grid, and it means nuclear. Italy shut its last two reactors in 1990, after a referendum in 1987, and voters turned down a restart in 2011. On 23 September the Senate gave final approval to a law to bring nuclear back, and the national energy plan puts new reactors around 2035.[^nuclear] That does nothing for this winter. It's how cheap energy stops depending on who is selling.

To be clear, defence spending did not put up anyone's gas bill. It is a budget choice that competes with hospitals, schools and tax cuts, and that argument deserves to be had honestly. But it is not what made September expensive.

## Why prices don't come back down

Inflation slowing down doesn't give anyone their money back. The big rises of 2021–23 stay in the price of rent, food, services and everything a business pays for. That's why "inflation is back near 2%" never felt like relief.

Then there's the question the left asks, and it's a fair one. Did companies use the chaos to raise prices by more than their costs?

In Italy the national accounts say yes, for a while. They can split the rise in the price of everything Italy produces, the GDP deflator, into what went to pay, what went to profits and the income of the self-employed, and what went to taxes. From 2021 to 2023, profits and self-employed income account for about 70% of the rise. Pay accounts for 18%. In the euro area as a whole the split was 53% and 35%.[^deflator]

Two things keep this from being a story about greed. First, look at what's in that line. It includes the earnings of energy producers in a year of windfalls, and the income of every self-employed person in the country. In Italy that means a lot of shopkeepers, bar owners and tradespeople. It even includes the rent that statisticians count for people who own their homes. The accounts can't separate the giants from the corner shop. Second, look at 2024. The profit share turned negative while pay caught up. Isabella Weber and Evan Wasner describe the mechanism: when a shock hits a whole sector at once, firms with market power can raise prices knowing their competitors will do the same, and the rise travels down the supply chain.[^weber] Ettore Gallo and Louis-Philippe Rochon call it real but temporary.[^gallo] That's what the Italian numbers look like.

Weber and Wasner also make the point about the small end: small businesses are price takers. When their costs rise they often can't pass them on, and when interest rates go up to fight inflation, they get hit harder than the giants.[^weber] The sensible response is to watch margins closely in energy, fuel distribution and banking. It is not price controls on the whole economy.

What about the idea that the ECB printed too much money? Years of cheap money did push up the price of assets, which helped the people who already owned them. Thomas Ferguson and Servaas Storm argue that in the United States, the wealth gains of the richest tenth of households, more than government stimulus, powered the demand surge of 2021.[^ferguson] But stopping the presses doesn't produce gas or apartments. Their bigger point is the one that matters here. In a world of wars, pandemics and rival powers, supply shocks will keep coming, and central banks can only fight them at enormous cost. They need targeted answers.

## Thirty years of flat wages

Energy explains this autumn. It doesn't explain why it hurts so much. For that you have to go back thirty years.

Of the 24 OECD countries with wage data back to 1990, only two pay a lower average real wage today than they did then: Japan, about 1% lower, and Italy, about 2%. Across the OECD, the average real wage rose by more than a third.[^oecdwages] At the start of 2024, Italian real wages were 6.9% below where they had been before the pandemic, the biggest fall among the large economies.[^oecd2024] This spring, contractual wages fell behind prices again, for the first time in ten quarters.[^istatwages]

The uncomfortable part is productivity. Output per hour worked in Italy has grown by about 8% since 1995. In the euro area it grew by 30%, in Germany by 35%.[^productivity] When output per hour doesn't grow, wages can't grow much for long.

  {...bw.productivity}

Which comes first is a real argument. The usual account blames too little investment, too many very small firms that never grow, slow courts and permits, and slow uptake of new technology. Another account runs the other way. Paolo Angelone and Rosaria Rita Canale find that, over sixty years of Italian data, a falling share of income going to wages goes with slower productivity growth: cheap labour let firms skip the investment that would have made them more productive.[^angelone] Either way, it's a trap. Many firms can't afford to pay more, low pay makes it harder to keep good people, and demand stays weak.

That's why I don't buy the idea that all the missing wage money is sitting in billionaires' accounts. Some of it went into profits in particular years and sectors, as above. A lot of it was never produced in the first place. Italy has a distribution problem, but it also has a production problem, and you can't march your way out of the second one.

## Milan's rent

Then there's Milan.

Italy as a whole is not in a housing bubble. Across the country, house prices relative to incomes sit at about 89% of their long-run average.[^oecdhouse] The stress is among renters. In 2025, 22.3% of Italians renting at market prices spent more than 40% of their disposable income on housing, against 18.6% across the EU. For owners with a mortgage the Italian figure was 1.3%, against 5.0%.[^overburden]

Milan is the extreme case. Researchers at the Politecnico di Milano tracked the city from 2015, the year of the Expo, to 2022. Average house prices rose 50%. Rents on new leases rose about a third. Average net income rose 18%, and the average wage about 13%. For blue-collar and white-collar workers, together 61% of the people working in the city, wages rose 9 to 10%. House prices grew about five times faster than their pay.[^bricocoli] A third of Milan's taxpayers earn less than €15,000 a year before tax, and more than half earn less than €26,000. Even the leases got shorter: contracts of one to three years went from 17.5% of new leases to 27.2%.

Cassa Depositi e Prestiti, the state-controlled lender, puts the rent on a 60-square-metre flat in Milan at about 76% of the average net wage in the province, against 65% in Rome and 48% in Bologna.[^cdp]

Why? Partly because Milan is doing well. When a city attracts jobs and can't add homes fast enough, the boom turns into rent. Economists at the Bank of Italy studied about a hundred Italian cities over forty years and found exactly that. Where housing supply can't expand easily, a jump in demand for workers raises house prices more and creates fewer jobs.[^accetturo] More mortgage credit pushes prices up too, most of all where supply is tight.[^barone] The same logic applies to any subsidy handed to buyers or renters in a tight market: part of it, sometimes most of it, ends up in the price. The Politecnico researchers warn about this directly. In a market that can't expand, higher wages or tax breaks for workers get absorbed by higher rents and prices.[^bricocoli]

Land is a big part of the problem. Across Italy, house prices tripled in real terms from the 1970s to the financial crisis of 2008, and about two-thirds of that came from rising land values.[^bricocoli] A study of a social housing project in Pordenone found that to reach the families who need it most, the building land would have had to cost about 30% less.[^berto] Add very little social housing, and decades of policy that favoured owning over renting.[^bricocoli]

Then there are short-term rentals. In September, Milan had 22,309 whole homes listed on Airbnb, and 16,177 of them had taken at least one booking with a review in the past year. They cluster in the centre: around the Duomo there are more than 500 per square kilometre. And this is not mostly people renting out a spare flat. Hosts with at least two whole homes on the site hold 63% of them, and hosts with ten or more hold 41%.[^airbnb]

"Ban Airbnb" alone doesn't fix Milan, and neither does "just build". It needs both more homes and a limit on how many of them go to tourists.

## The shop behind the plywood

Back to the windows.

Small businesses are squeezed from both sides. Their costs go up, and their customers cut back on everything that isn't a necessity. In the first half of 2026, Italy's smallest business users of electricity paid 38.9 euro cents a kilowatt-hour before VAT, about a third more than the EU average of 28.7. The largest users paid 34% of what the smallest did, a wider gap than the EU's 39%.[^business] And in 2025, 29.4% of Italian families said they had cut the quantity or quality of the food they buy, down from 31.1% the year before.[^istatspend]

The owner behind the plywood didn't set this autumn's prices. They are paying them twice: once on their own bill, and again in the customers who stopped coming. Then someone smashed their window in the name of the cost of living.

## What the government did, and what it can't do

This is the side dish, so I'll keep it short. The fair way to judge any government is by what it actually controls.

It doesn't control the price of gas, or whether a strait in the Gulf is open. It inherited thirty years of flat productivity and wages. What it controls is the response: how it helps people through the shock, how it taxes work, how fast it lets cities build, and how quickly it cuts the country's dependence on imported energy, which still covered 74% of Italy's needs in 2024, against 57% for the EU.[^dependency]

On the first, the record is better than the placards say. Researchers at the University of Florence and the Parliamentary Budget Office simulated the 2022 shock on Italian households. Without any help, prices would have raised household spending by about 9.6% on average, and by about 19% for the poorest tenth. Discounts on bills and cash transfers brought the average down to 5.1%, and the poorest tenth ended up at 2.6%, below the average. The targeted transfers did most of the work. When the help was scaled back in 2023, the effect tilted back against the poorest.[^bardazzi] Two governments in a row ran that support. The current one also made its cut in taxes on work permanent.[^wedge]

The room to do more is small. Italy's public debt is among the highest in Europe. A 2024 model of the Italian economy found no painless path out of the last shock. If inflation had stuck around 5%, or if EU rules had forced a fast cut in the deficit, Italy would have fallen into recession and its debt would have grown anyway.[^canelli] "Just spend more" comes with a bill too.

What is fair to criticise is that the support was temporary and didn't change the things that made Italy so exposed. Italy still takes 45.8% of the cost of employing a single worker on the average wage in tax and contributions. That is the fifth-highest in the OECD, above the EU average of 41.7% and far above the OECD average of 35.1%.[^taxwedge]

But this runs into the same debt. With debt this high, a cut in taxes on work has to be paid for, by taxing something else more or by spending less somewhere else. Otherwise it is just more debt. As with energy, something has to give.

After the riots, the government sent soldiers to Milan and passed the emergency decree, with up to five years in prison for violent participation in a demonstration.[^decree] You can argue about whether it goes too far. Parliament has sixty days to turn it into law, and judges will decide how it is applied. That argument happens in parliament and in court, which is the point of the section on fascism above.

## Claims on both sides

The migration row deserves its numbers. ## What would actually help

None of this means nothing can be done. It means the useful things are less exciting than a riot.

**Help people, not prices.** When the next spike comes, pay income-tested credits on bills instead of cutting fuel taxes for everyone. Universal cuts cost a fortune, help the biggest users most and weaken the reason to save energy. The 2022 numbers show targeted transfers doing most of the work.[^bardazzi] Small businesses that are viable but use a lot of energy can get temporary help too.

**Need less imported gas.** Build more renewables, storage, grid and links to neighbouring countries, get the new reactors built, and keep diversified gas contracts while we get there. Change how cheap power is sold, too. With long-term contracts for renewable plants, their output stops being priced as if it came from a gas plant. And small users should stop paying so much more per unit than big ones.

**Watch the sectors with power.** Monitor margins in energy, fuel and food distribution and banking. If there are windfall profits, tax them with clear rules set in advance, not as punishment after the fact.[^weber] No general price controls. They bring shortages, worse quality and hidden rationing.

**Raise pay by making work more productive.** Renew wage contracts on time. If Italy wants a minimum wage, have an independent commission set it from the data, alongside collective bargaining, not instead of it. The fear that it all comes back in prices is overdone. In US supermarkets, a 10% rise in the minimum wage lifted grocery prices by 0.6% to 0.8%.[^leung] Across 29 OECD countries in the 2021–24 inflation, a 10% rise lifted overall prices by about 0.3% within five months.[^bilenkisi] Then do the slow work: let firms grow without hitting rules that punish size, speed up civil courts and permits, invest in training and technology, and make hiring cheaper.

**Tax work less, and pay for it.** Shift the weight onto property, inheritance and pollution, and close low-value exemptions, so the cut doesn't add to the debt.

**Build in Milan.** More homes near the metro and the trains, approval deadlines that actually hold, more social and cost-rent housing, empty flats brought back to the market, and limits on short-term rentals in the neighbourhoods where they have taken over. Skip subsidies for buyers, because in a tight market they end up in the price.[^barone] Let the city capture some of the land value it creates.[^bricocoli]

**Pay for defence honestly.** Fund it over a credible plan of several years, buy real capability together with European partners, cut waste and low-value tax breaks first, and stop pretending it is either free or the cause of the bill.

## What it adds up to

The plywood will come down. Glass is cheap compared with what it was covering.

The people in the street are right that life got harder. They are wrong about why. And the ones who broke windows made it harder still for exactly the people the march said it was for.

Energy started this autumn's shock. Thirty years of weak wages made it hurt. Milan's housing made it worse. Profits ran ahead for two years, and then fell back. And none of it was done by a regime, because there isn't one.

The answer isn't revolution, and it isn't pretending everything is fine. It's a long list of unglamorous things: less imported gas, more productive firms, more homes in Milan, taxes that fall less on work, and help aimed at the people who need it. It doesn't fit on a placard. It's much harder to argue with.
