In the span of seven days in mid-September 2026, three announcements landed that would have each dominated a news cycle on their own.

On Monday, the Potsdam Institute published its annual Planetary Health Check: seven of nine planetary boundaries — the guardrails scientists use to define a safe operating space for civilization — are transgressed at the worst levels ever recorded.[^phc] On Tuesday, Copernicus reported that the Antarctic ozone hole had ballooned to 25 million square kilometers, five million more than the seasonal average.[^ozone] And on Wednesday, a satellite study spanning nearly five decades confirmed that Greenland and Antarctica have shed 11.3 trillion metric tonnes of ice since 1979 — enough meltwater to submerge the continental United States under a meter and a half.[^imbie]

Meanwhile, a Super El Niño is shattering observational records in the Pacific.[^nino] August was the hottest month in recorded history.[^august] S&P Global declared the 1.5°C warming target unachievable under any plausible scenario.[^sp] And in Nepal, a mountainside destabilized by decades of glacial thinning collapsed and killed over 1,300 people.[^wwa]

None of these stories is isolated. All of them are chapters in the same narrative — one the science has been writing for decades. Here's what the evidence actually says about each, and where it's still genuinely uncertain.

## The scorecard: seven out of nine, and the last holdout just fell

The planetary boundaries framework originated in a landmark 2009 paper by Johan Rockström and colleagues, who proposed nine Earth-system thresholds that humanity shouldn't cross if we want to keep the planet in the relatively stable state that allowed civilizations to develop.[^rockstrom2009] Think of them as the vital signs on a planetary check-up: climate change, biodiversity loss, ocean acidification, ozone depletion, nitrogen and phosphorus flows, land-use change, freshwater use, aerosol loading, and chemical pollution.

The 2009 paper itself was candid that the proposed thresholds were rough first estimates with large uncertainties. That honesty hasn't stopped the framework from becoming one of the most influential concepts in environmental science — or from generating fierce debate about how exactly you operationalize a "boundary" and translate it into policy.

By the 2023 update, led by Katherine Richardson and published in Science Advances, six of the nine boundaries were transgressed.[^richardson2023] Ocean acidification was the stubborn holdout — close to the line, but not formally across it.

Not anymore. A 2025 reassessment using revised preindustrial baselines and better observational data concluded that surface ocean acidification had already crossed into the boundary's uncertainty range by 2020, with more than 40% of the surface ocean and up to 60% of the ocean down to 200 meters now past the threshold.[^findlay2025] Habitat losses linked to current conditions include 43% for tropical and subtropical coral reefs and up to 61% for polar pteropods — the tiny sea snails that form a critical base of marine food webs.

So the September 2026 Planetary Health Check now counts seven transgressions, all at their worst recorded levels. Only stratospheric ozone (which is recovering, more on that in a moment) and aerosol loading remain within bounds.

The debate among scientists isn't really about whether these pressures exist. It's about implementation: which control variables best capture each boundary, how to handle regional variation, and whether the framework helps or hurts when it gets into policy rooms. The van Vuuren team, in a 2025 pathways analysis, demonstrated that swapping one metric for another — pH instead of aragonite saturation for ocean acidification, PM2.5 instead of aerosol optical depth for aerosol loading — materially changes the policy implications.[^vanvuuren2025] The framework is powerful but not precise, which is exactly what its creators said from the start.

What the Potsdam team's lead author Boris Sakschewski put bluntly: the risk of large-scale, persistent, and potentially irreversible changes is growing, while the margin for error is shrinking.[^sakschewski] That's not a framing. It's a reading of the data.

## The bright spot (sort of): ozone is healing, just erratically

If you want evidence that global environmental agreements can actually work, the Antarctic ozone layer is your exhibit A.

The Montreal Protocol, signed exactly 39 years ago this week, phased out the chlorofluorocarbons and other chemicals that were tearing a hole in Earth's UV shield every austral spring.[^montreal] Ozone-depleting substances in the stratosphere peaked and began declining. A 2017 detection study found that the ozone mass deficit — a measure combining hole size and depth — had dropped by about 30% since the peak.[^delaat] A 2025 fingerprinting study in Nature achieved the strongest statistical confirmation yet: observed changes in ozone by month and altitude match the pattern you'd expect from declining halogens.[^wang2025] The signal is real.

<Aside label="the date">
  Signed 16 September 1987. Thirty-nine years on, the anniversary fell this month.
</Aside>

So why, in September 2026, is the ozone hole the size of North America?

Because recovery isn't a straight line. Year-to-year variability is driven by meteorology — specifically, stratospheric temperatures over the South Pole that control the formation of polar stratospheric clouds. Those clouds provide surfaces for the chemical reactions that activate chlorine radicals, which then shred ozone molecules when springtime sunlight returns. An unusually cold stratosphere in early September 2026 triggered rapid cloud formation, and the hole expanded fast.

Copernicus was careful to point out what the area number doesn't tell you. While the hole's extent exceeded the seasonal average by five million square kilometers, the minimum ozone column and the ozone mass deficit both remained near historical norms. In plain English: the hole was wide but not unusually deep.

This distinction matters and often gets lost in headlines. Scientists use multiple metrics — hole area, minimum column ozone, and mass deficit — precisely because recovery timing differs depending on which one you track. A big hole in a given year doesn't mean recovery has reversed. The large holes of 2020–2022, for instance, were linked to springtime weather patterns, wildfire aerosol, and volcanic particles — not to a resurgence of ozone-depleting chemicals.[^holes]

The honest summary: the Montreal Protocol is working, full ozone-hole disappearance is still expected around 2065, and the bumpy ride between now and then will keep generating alarming-looking September headlines.[^wmo] The question researchers are still actively working on is how much recent wildfire chemistry and dynamical variability are delaying the apparent recovery timeline.

One important caveat the current evidence set can't settle: the Kigali Amendment's widely cited claim of avoiding up to 0.5°C of warming by targeting HFCs. It's plausible and comes from UN estimates, but a peer-reviewed quantified assessment supporting that specific number isn't present in the recent literature.[^kigali] Worth noting when that figure shows up in policy discussions.

## The quiet emergency: Earth's carbon sponge is drying out

This is the story that should be getting more attention than it does.

Every year, land ecosystems and oceans absorb roughly half of humanity's CO₂ emissions. Forests, soils, and seawater act as a giant carbon sponge, slowing the rate at which greenhouse gases accumulate in the atmosphere. If that sponge weakens, the same level of emissions produces faster warming. Simple math, terrifying implications.

The most comprehensive recent synthesis, published in Nature in 2024 by Yude Pan and colleagues, found that the global forest carbon sink has remained roughly stable over three decades — but only because gains in some biomes masked devastating losses in others.[^pan2024] Boreal forests lost 36% of their sink capacity. Tropical intact forests lost 31%. And two-thirds of the climate benefit from the remaining forest sink was negated by tropical deforestation, running at 2.2 billion tonnes of carbon per year over 1990–2019.

Then came 2023, and the numbers went from concerning to alarming. A rapid assessment published in National Science Review found that the global net land sink collapsed to just 0.44 billion tonnes of carbon — the weakest since 2003.[^ke2024] The CO₂ growth rate at Mauna Loa hit 3.37 parts per million, 86% higher than the previous year. The Amazon drought alone drove 0.31 billion tonnes in abnormal losses. Canadian wildfires contributed 0.58 billion tonnes. Land uptake north of 20°N — where boreal and temperate forests do the bulk of their work — had halved since 2015.

Independent research in Nature Ecology & Evolution confirmed the mechanism: warm extremes weaken carbon uptake primarily by reducing gross primary productivity — photosynthesis, essentially — with tropical regions contributing about 81% of the effect.[^yuan2024] The culprit is increasingly vapor pressure deficit (how thirsty the air is), which matters more than temperature or soil moisture alone. When the air gets that dry, plants close their stomata to conserve water, and carbon absorption drops.

Is this a temporary blip from an extreme year, or the start of a persistent decline? That's the central unresolved question, and the honest answer is: we don't know yet. Scenario modeling projects that the terrestrial sink saturates under higher warming pathways, with stronger interannual swings and a net negative climate contribution by late century.[^ipcc] A 2025 U.S. assessment found more years functioning as a carbon source than sink since 2000, with CO₂ fertilization — the theoretical boost from plants growing faster in a CO₂-rich atmosphere — proving insufficient to maintain sink strength.[^liu2025]

</Aside>

What the Potsdam team framed sharply: our pathway to net-zero emissions assumes the carbon sinks keep working. Current observations suggest that assumption may be optimistic.

## 11.3 trillion tonnes and counting

The IMBIE study — the most comprehensive satellite analysis of polar ice loss ever conducted — brought a number into focus that's easy to read and hard to process: 11.3 trillion metric tonnes of ice, gone since 1979.

The team, led by Inès Otosaka and Andrew Shepherd at the University of Northumbria, combined 42 independent satellite surveys from 27 missions. One of the most striking findings was about how the ice is disappearing. Eighty-four percent of the combined loss wasn't from surface melting. It was from glaciers accelerating and dumping larger volumes of ice directly into the ocean. The trend is driven by warming ocean water eating into the glaciers from below — a process that's harder to observe and harder to reverse than surface melt.

The rates tell the acceleration story. Greenland's losses climbed from about 60 billion tonnes per year in the 1980s to 264 billion in the 2010s. Antarctica went from 48 billion to 202 billion over the same period. A temporary slowdown between 2020 and 2023 — driven by heavy snowfall on East Antarctica and milder Greenland summers — offered a brief reprieve, but scientists caution against reading it as a trend change.

Total contribution to sea-level rise: about 3.1 centimeters since 1979. That's a number that sounds small until you translate it into people — an additional six to nine million exposed to coastal flooding and erosion.

## The wildcard: a record El Niño heading into winter

As if the baseline trends weren't enough, a Super El Niño is now intensifying in the equatorial Pacific at a pace that's left climate scientists reaching for superlatives.

By September 20, the Niño 3.4 sea-surface temperature anomaly hit 3.05°C above the 1991–2020 average — surpassing the previous all-time record of 3.02°C set at the peak of the 2015–16 event, and this one is still months from its expected peak. NOAA puts the probability of it becoming the strongest El Niño since at least 1950 above 90%.[^noaa]

El Niño matters far beyond the Pacific. Strong events amplify planetary wave patterns that send energy crashing into the stratosphere, weakening the polar vortex — the band of winds that normally keeps Arctic air bottled up over the pole. Seasonal models from both the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts and the UK Met Office are flagging a likely sudden stratospheric warming event around January–February 2027, which would shove Arctic air south over North America and Europe with a typical 10- to 30-day lag.[^ssw] The 2015–16 Super El Niño produced exactly this pattern.

The compound effect is what matters. A stronger El Niño redistributes heat globally, pushes the CO₂ growth rate higher (partly by stressing tropical vegetation), and loads the dice for extreme weather at both poles and in the midlatitudes. August 2026 already tied July 2023 as the hottest month in the instrumental record, at 1.65°C above preindustrial — the first month to breach the 1.5°C line since November 2025.

## The number that died: 1.5°C

S&P Global's September report delivered a verdict that many climate scientists had quietly accepted but few institutions had been willing to state flatly: limiting warming to 1.5°C is no longer achievable under any plausible trajectory. Energy demand in emerging economies is projected to grow by over 60% by 2060 — the equivalent of adding another China to the global energy system — outrunning decarbonization even in aggressive scenarios.

This doesn't make the Paris Agreement irrelevant. Every tenth of a degree still matters for the frequency and severity of extremes, the survival of ecosystems, and the displacement of people. But it does mean the policy conversation needs to shift from "can we stay under 1.5?" (no) to "how do we limit overshoot, and what does adaptation look like at 1.7 or 2.0?"

## The human cost, written in a Himalayan mountainside

On August 26, a mountainside above Langtang Lirung in Nepal gave way and killed more than 1,300 people. The World Weather Attribution analysis, published three weeks later, found that decades of warming had thinned glaciers and thawed permafrost deep in the rock, progressively destabilizing the slope. A 7.8-magnitude earthquake in 2015 may have weakened it further, but the warming contribution was the long fuse.

Nearly 200 glacial lakes in India alone are now classified as high-risk. Only 21 of the roughly 40,000 glaciers in the Hindu Kush–Himalaya are monitored on the ground. The region is approaching what hydrologists call "peak water" — the point at which glacial melt begins declining not because the situation improves, but because there's less ice left to melt — potentially by mid-century.[^lakes]

## What it adds up to

There's a temptation, with a week like this, to either catastrophize or shrug. The science does neither.

What the evidence shows clearly is that Earth's buffering systems — the ozone layer, the carbon sinks, the ice sheets, the stable boundaries that kept conditions hospitable — are under compound stress. Some are recovering (ozone). Some are holding but fraying (carbon sinks). Some are accelerating in the wrong direction (ice, acidification, biodiversity). And a record El Niño is about to stress-test all of them simultaneously.

The uncertainties are real. Whether the 2023 carbon sink collapse was a spike or a trend remains unresolved. How much wildfire chemistry is altering ozone recovery timelines is active research. Model projections for ice-sheet dynamics carry wide error bars. None of this is settled, and pretending otherwise would be bad science.

But the direction is settled. Seven of nine planetary boundaries are at their worst recorded levels. The margin between "manageable risk" and "irreversible change" is narrower than it was a year ago, and narrower than it was a decade ago. The systems aren't independent — they're coupled, and stress in one propagates to others in ways that are easier to model in theory than to predict in practice.

Rockström, from the Potsdam Institute, put it in terms that are worth repeating not because they're comforting but because they're accurate: we don't have the luxury of time, but we do have the knowledge, the capacity, and the solutions to change course.[^rockstrom]

<PullQuote by="Johan Rockström">We don't have the luxury of time, but we do have the knowledge, the capacity, and the solutions to change course.</PullQuote>

September 2026 is telling us that the window for using them is not infinite.
